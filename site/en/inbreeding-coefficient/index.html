    Racing Pigeon Inbreeding Coefficient Testing: Inbreeding Risk and AB-Pigeon Identification¶

    Key takeaway: The racing pigeon inbreeding coefficient (F) measures how homozygous a bird's genome is and is the key indicator for assessing inbreeding depression risk and guiding pairings. Testing homozygosity across a large number of SNP loci quantifies how closely bred a bird is and supports mating plans that avoid close relatives; the same loci also power AB-pigeon identification, adding another DNA line of defence for pedigree integrity and race fairness.

    In racing pigeon breeding, inbreeding is a double-edged sword — moderate inbreeding fixes desirable traits, excessive inbreeding triggers inbreeding depression. Inbreeding coefficient testing turns that judgement call into quantifiable data, while AB-pigeon testing protects identity itself against same-name substitution; together they safeguard the integrity of a bloodline library.

    What the racing pigeon inbreeding coefficient (F) is¶

    The inbreeding coefficient, also called the coefficient of inbreeding (F), is the probability that the two alleles at a locus in an individual are identical by descent — that is, they come from the same copy in a common ancestor. F runs from 0 to 1 (often expressed as a percentage): the higher the value, the more closely related the parents and the more homozygous the genome.

    Theoretical inbreeding coefficients for common mating types:

    Mating type Inbreeding coefficient F Notes
    Parent × offspring 25% Father × daughter, mother × son
    Full siblings 25% Brother and sister from the same parents
    Half siblings 12.5% Same sire, different dams, or the reverse
    First cousins 6.25% Uncle–niece, aunt–nephew, cousins

    Note that the table gives theoretical values. A real bird's inbreeding level accumulates across repeated close matings in its pedigree, so only DNA testing can assess it accurately.

    How it works: using DNA testing to quantify inbreeding¶

    The immediate consequence of a close mating is rising genome-wide homozygosity and falling heterozygosity. A laboratory can therefore estimate inbreeding by typing the homozygous/heterozygous state of a large number of SNP loci (single nucleotide polymorphisms):

    1. Locus typing: genotype from tens to hundreds of SNP loci
    2. Homozygosity calculation: count homozygous loci as a proportion of all loci
    3. Coefficient estimation: the higher the homozygosity, the larger F and the closer the breeding

    This logic follows directly from the homozygous/heterozygous genotype concepts in the genetics basics for racing pigeons. Compared with estimates from pedigree records, DNA testing reflects the genome's true homozygosity and is particularly useful for flocks with incomplete records.

    Weighing the pros and cons of close pairing¶

    Aspect Benefit Cost
    Desirable traits Fast fixation of bloodline strengths Higher risk of exposing harmful recessives
    Reproductive capacity — Lower fertility and hatch rate
    Immunity — Weaker disease resistance (inbreeding depression)
    Vigour and performance — Frail constitution, slow growth, unstable homing

    Moderate inbreeding (line-breeding, for example) is one breeding tool, but it must be preceded by inbreeding coefficient testing so that the level of inbreeding stays within a safe range and inbreeding depression is avoided.

    AB pigeons and individual identification: another line of defence¶

    In the context of pedigree management, an AB pigeon is a bird used in an identity-fraud scheme: several similar-looking pigeons share one ring identity, with pigeon A racing and pigeon B resting, switched back and forth to keep the "entry" in peak condition.

    Sanshi Bio uses 58 genetic loci for individual identification:

    1. Build a gene ID card for each entry before the race (a 58-SNP-locus profile)
    2. Re-sample and compare winning birds after the race
    3. A locus mismatch indicates substitution

    DNA is the one identifier every pigeon is born with — impossible to forge and impossible to substitute. AB-pigeon testing works alongside paternity testing to protect both identity and pedigree, safeguarding race fairness and the integrity of bloodlines.

    Running the tests: practical workflow¶

    Inbreeding coefficient testing:

    1. Sampling: pull 4–6 breast or belly feathers with intact follicles
    2. Submission: send the samples to the Sanshi Bio laboratory
    3. Typing: test the homozygous/heterozygous state of multiple SNP loci
    4. Reporting: an inbreeding coefficient assessment plus pairing advice for avoiding close relatives

    AB-pigeon verification:

    1. Pre-race profiling: build a 58-locus genetic profile for each entry
    2. Sample archiving: retain feather samples on file
    3. Post-race verification: re-sample and compare winning birds
    4. Conclusion: matching loci confirm identity; mismatches indicate substitution

    💡 Tip: when importing stock, run paternity testing and inbreeding coefficient testing together to confirm the pedigree and assess inbreeding risk; one-loft races and clubs can build profiles before the race and verify afterwards to shut out AB-pigeon cheating. For the full technical overview see the home page of this knowledge base.

    Key points¶

    1. The inbreeding coefficient F quantifies genomic homozygosity — DNA testing is more direct and accurate than pedigree estimates.
    2. Moderate inbreeding fixes traits; excessive inbreeding causes depression — falling fertility and immunity.
    3. Parent–offspring and full-sibling matings give F ≈ 25%; half siblings 12.5%; first cousins 6.25%.
    4. Plan pairings to avoid close relatives — use the inbreeding coefficient to design matings and control risk.
    5. AB pigeons are a same-name substitution fraud — DNA individual identification leaves no hiding place.

    FAQ¶

    What are the risks of pairing close relatives?¶

    Close pairing raises homozygosity. On one hand it can fix desirable traits, but more importantly it exposes harmful recessive genes and causes inbreeding depression — lower fertility, poor hatch rates, weaker disease resistance, and less robust constitution and homing stability. Parent–offspring or full-sibling matings carry the highest risk.

    What does "AB pigeon" mean?¶

    An AB pigeon is a "same-name substitute" — two similar-looking birds sharing one ring identity, one racing while the other rests, switched frequently to cheat. DNA individual identification (comparison of 58 loci) can detect the substitution.

    What inbreeding coefficient is normal for a racing pigeon?¶

    There is no universal "normal" value; it depends on your breeding goal. Outbred birds typically sit close to F = 0, closely bred birds show a clearly raised F, and parent–offspring or full-sibling offspring sit at F ≈ 25%. Keeping inbreeding at a low level is generally advised.

    Is it acceptable to pair close relatives?¶

    Yes, but with care. Moderate line-breeding is a tool for fixing desirable traits; test the inbreeding coefficient first to assess risk, avoid continuing to breed from animals with a high F, and watch for signs of inbreeding depression.

    What is the difference between inbreeding coefficient testing and paternity testing?¶

    Paternity testing confirms which bird a pigeon's parents are; inbreeding coefficient testing measures how homozygous (how closely bred) that pigeon's genome is. Used together they give a full picture of pedigree and inbreeding risk.

    Can the inbreeding coefficient be tested if pedigree records are incomplete?¶

    Yes. DNA testing analyses genome-wide homozygosity directly and does not depend on complete pedigrees, which makes it especially suitable for flocks with patchy records or unknown origins.

    Does AB-pigeon testing require a pre-race profile?¶

    Yes. A 58-locus gene ID card must be built for each race entry beforehand, and winning birds re-sampled and compared afterwards, before a substitution can be determined.
